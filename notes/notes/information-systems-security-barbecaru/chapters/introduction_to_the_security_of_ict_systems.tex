\section{What is computer/information
security?}
\subsection{Definition and security properties}
At its core, \textbf{computer security} (often used interchangeably with \textbf{ICT security} or \textbf{Information security}) is the practice of protecting information and the systems that process, store, and transmit it. Rather than just a set of tools like firewalls or antivirus software, it is a continuous process of managing risk by enforcing specific security properties.
Today, security involves ensuring that data remains accurate, accessible, and private, regardless of whether it is at rest on a server or in transit across a global network. These goals are achieved by implementing policies and mechanisms that uphold a set of fundamental properties.
The most critical of these properties are summarized in the following table.
\begin{table}[h!]
\centering
\caption{Security Properties and Definitions}
\label{tab:security_properties}
\begin{tabular}{|l|p{10cm}|}
\hline
\textbf{Property} & \textbf{Definition} \\ \hline
Confidentiality & Ensures that information is not made available or disclosed to unauthorized individuals, entities, or processes. \\ \hline
Authentication & Verifies the identity of a user, process, or device. \textbf{Peer entity authentication} verifies the identity of one entity in an association; \textbf{Mutual authentication} requires both parties to verify each other. \\ \hline
Integrity & Maintains the consistency, accuracy, and trustworthiness of data. It protects against unauthorized \textbf{modification}, \textbf{deletion}, or the \textbf{replay} of captured valid messages. \\ \hline
Authorization & The process of granting or denying specific access rights or privileges to an entity after successful authentication. \\ \hline
Non-repudiation & Provides proof of the origin or delivery of data so that the sender or receiver cannot later deny having processed that data. \\ \hline
Availability & Ensures that systems and data are accessible and usable upon demand by an authorized entity. \\ \hline
Accountability & Requirement that the actions of an entity be traced uniquely to that entity, supporting non-repudiation, fault isolation, and intrusion detection. \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{CIA Triad}

The \textbf{CIA Triad} is a model designed to guide policies for information security within an organization. It focuses on the three core pillars: \textbf{Confidentiality}, \textbf{Integrity}, and \textbf{Availability}. These three concepts are often viewed as a balancing act; increasing the strength of one property can sometimes lead to a decrease in another.

In practical implementation, there is an inherent trade-off among these properties. For example:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Confidentiality vs. Availability:} Implementing strict multi-factor authentication and heavy encryption protects sensitive data (Confidentiality) but may slow down legitimate users or prevent access during a system failure (Availability).
    \item \textbf{Integrity vs. Availability:} Rigorous checksums and verification processes ensure that data remains unaltered (Integrity), but the computational overhead required for these checks can increase latency and reduce system responsiveness (Availability).
\end{itemize}

Finding the right balance depends on the specific \textbf{threat model} and the requirements of the system being protected.

\subsection{Evolution of ICT Models: Centralized vs. Distributed}

The architecture of ICT systems has evolved significantly, shifting the primary focus of security strategies from physical perimeters to data-centric protection.
\subsubsection*{Centralized Model}
In the early days of computing, systems followed a \textbf{centralized model}. In this scenario, a powerful central server (mainframe) was accessed by remote "dumb" terminals. Security was relatively straightforward, relying on two main pillars:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical Protection:} Restricting physical access to the server room prevented unauthorized tampering.
    \item \textbf{Logical Protection:} Basic authentication and authorization mechanisms controlled user access to the central resources.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection*{Distributed Model}
Modern systems have transitioned to a \textbf{distributed model}, where numerous nodes (PCs, mobile devices, data servers, IoT devices, and routers) are spread across various geographical locations. These nodes communicate over diverse mediums—including WiFi, Ethernet, and Satellite—using a complex stack of protocols (e.g., IP, TCP, UDP).
In this environment, the "perimeter" has disappeared. Security must now follow the data, making \textbf{confidentiality} and \textbf{integrity} mandatory not just at the endpoints, but specifically for **data in transit**.

\subsubsection*{Modern Security Challenges}
The complexity of the distributed model introduces several persistent vulnerabilities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Insecure Protocols:} Many legacy protocols still in use (e.g., DHCP, ARP) were designed for trust and lack inherent security or authentication.
    \item \textbf{Weak Authentication:} Reliance on simple password-based systems remains a significant risk factor.
    \item \textbf{Software Complexity:} As programs become more complex, they inevitably contain bugs that can be exploited.
    \item \textbf{Persistent Malware:} The interconnected nature of nodes facilitates the rapid spread of viruses, worms, and specialized malware.
\end{itemize}

\section{Risk Management}

\subsection{The Complexity of Security}
The complexity of modern security is due to the broad diversity of devices and communication networks. A fundamental principle in this field is that \textbf{a system is only as secure as its weakest link}. As services become increasingly distributed and software grows in complexity, the number of potential entry points for an attacker increases exponentially.

\subsection{Terminology}
To manage risk effectively, we must define the relationship between weaknesses and actions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vulnerability:} A technical or operational weakness that could be exploited by an adversary.
    \item \textbf{Threat:} Any action, whether deliberate or accidental, that has the potential to cause harm to the system.
    \item \textbf{Attack:} A threat occurrence resulting from a deliberate, malicious action.
    \item \textbf{Negative Event:} A threat occurrence resulting from an accidental or natural event.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Risk Evaluation and the Risk Matrix}
Risk is quantified by evaluating two primary parameters: \textbf{Impact} (the severity of the consequences) and \textbf{Likelihood} (the probability of the event occurring).
The standard formula for identification is:
\[ \text{Risk} = \text{Impact} \times \text{Likelihood} \]

\subsection{Management Framework and Security Design}
The risk management framework generally follows a four-phase lifecycle: \textbf{Preparation}, \textbf{Evaluation}, \textbf{Implementation}, and \textbf{Monitoring}. This process is guided by three strategic pillars:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Security Policy:} Defining precisely \textit{what} needs to be achieved.
    \item \textbf{Threat Model:} Identifying \textit{who} the attackers are and their capabilities.
    \item \textbf{Defense Implementation:} Building a system that prevents attackers from violating the policy within the defined threat model.
\end{enumerate}

It is important to acknowledge that perfect security is impossible. All security is based on assumptions; if an assumption is wrong or an unforeseen scenario arises, the system remains at risk.

\subsection{Core Security Principles}
To mitigate these risks, three core principles should guide system design:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Simplicity (KISS Rule):} ``Keep It Simple, Stupid.'' Designs should be kept small and simple to remain easy to test, verify, and maintain.
    \item \textbf{Know Your Threat Model:} You cannot defend against everything. Understanding the goals and resources of your specific attackers is essential for prioritizing defenses.
    \item \textbf{Defense in Depth:} Implementing multiple, redundant layers of security so that the failure of one component does not lead to a total compromise. However, note that the total security of a system is often less than the sum of its parts due to integration complexities.
\end{itemize}

\section{Classes of Attacks}

Threats can be categorized based on the nature of the interaction with the target system:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Passive Attacks:} The attacker aims to obtain information without altering system resources. Victims are typically unaware of the intrusion (e.g., traffic sniffing).
    \item \textbf{Active Attacks:} The attacker attempts to alter system resources or affect their operation. These are often detectable because data is modified, deleted, or services are interrupted.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Packet Sniffing (Eavesdropping)}
Packet sniffing is a passive attack where an attacker reads data packets intended for other recipients. This is particularly effective in broadcast networks (like unencrypted WiFi or legacy Ethernet hubs). It becomes a critical threat when confidentiality is not enforced through encryption, allowing attackers to capture sensitive information like credentials or private communications.

\subsection{IP Spoofing}
In this active attack, the adversary modifies the source IP address in the packet header to impersonate another trusted system. Spoofing is a fundamental tool for hiding an attacker's identity and is frequently utilized in Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attacks to bypass basic IP-based filtering.

\subsection{Denial of Service (DoS)}
A DoS attack is an active attempt to make a server or network resource unavailable to its intended users. The goal is to keep the host so busy processing malicious requests that it cannot provide its legitimate service. Common methods include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Ping Bombing: Overwhelming a target with ICMP Echo Request packets.
    \item TCP SYN Flood: Exploiting the TCP three-way handshake to exhaust server resources.
    \item DNS Floods: Slighting a DNS server with a high volume of requests to disrupt name resolution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS)}
DDoS follows the same logic as DoS but leverages a massive number of compromised nodes, often called \textit{zombies} or \textit{daemons}, to launch a coordinated strike.

The typical lifecycle of a DDoS attack is as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Selection:} A target victim is chosen.
    \item \textbf{Infection:} Malware is installed on multiple vulnerable nodes globally.
    \item \textbf{Organization:} Specific zombies are designated as "masters" to coordinate the attack.
    \item \textbf{Command:} The attacker sends a start signal to the master nodes.
    \item \textbf{Execution:} Masters trigger the zombies to simultaneously flood the victim with traffic.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Shadow Server}
In a shadow server attack, the adversary successfully impersonates a legitimate service provider to the victim. While often used for data theft, it can also facilitate a Denial of Service by redirecting users to a non-functional or malicious clone of a service (e.g., a DNS Shadow Server). The primary countermeasure against this is robust \textbf{Server Authentication}.

\subsection{ARP Poisoning}
The Address Resolution Protocol (ARP) is used to map a Layer 3 (IP) address to a Layer 2 (MAC) address. Because ARP is stateless and lacks authentication, it is highly vulnerable to "poisoning" or "spoofing". An attacker sends fraudulent ARP messages onto a local area network to link their MAC address with the IP address of a legitimate node (like the default gateway).

\textbf{Example Attack Scenario:}
Consider a network with Alice (Victim, \texttt{.5}, MAC: \texttt{AA}), a Router (Gateway, \texttt{.1}, MAC: \texttt{BB}), and Eve (Attacker, \texttt{.10}, MAC: \texttt{CC}).
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initial State:} Alice's cache correctly maps the Router: \texttt{192.168.1.1} $\rightarrow$ \texttt{BB:BB:BB:BB:BB:BB}.
    \item \textbf{The Poisoning:} Eve sends an unsolicited ARP reply to Alice: \textit{``The IP 192.168.1.1 is at MAC CC:CC:CC:CC:CC:CC.''} Alice updates her table without verification.
    \item \textbf{The Result:} Alice's cache now reads: $192.168.1.1 \rightarrow$ \texttt{CC:CC:CC:CC:CC:CC}. All her outgoing internet traffic is now sent directly to Eve.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Man-in-the-Middle (MITM)}
A MITM attack occurs when an attacker sits between two communicating parties, gaining the ability to read, modify, or delete traffic in real-time without either party knowing. ARP poisoning is a common method used to establish a MITM position on a local network.

\section{Software Vulnerabilities and Malwares}

\subsection{Window of Exposure}
The \textbf{Window of Exposure} represents the total time a system is at risk due to a specific security vulnerability. This risk profile is typically divided into three primary phases:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Discovery Phase (Zero-Day):} This begins when a new vulnerability is discovered. During this time, an exploit may be created (the red dot in Figure \ref{fig:window_of_exposure}), but the vendor and the public remain unaware. Because no defense exists, this is a \textbf{zero-day} period; risk levels rise sharply as the vulnerability becomes publicly known.
    \item \textbf{Publication Phase (Unpatched):} Once the vendor is informed and the vulnerability is made public, the risk peaks. While security tools like Intrusion Detection Systems (IDS) may be updated with temporary signatures, the system remains \textbf{unpatched} and vulnerable to any actor aware of the flaw.
    \item \textbf{Protection Phase (Patched):} Risk drops significantly once a formal \textbf{patch is published}. The window finally "closes" once the patch is successfully \textbf{installed} on the target system, returning the risk level to its baseline.
\end{enumerate}

The primary goal of security management is to "shrink" this window by accelerating vulnerability identification and reducing the time required for patch deployment.

\subsection{Common Types of Malware}
Malware is a broad category of malicious software designed to compromise the security properties of a system. Common types include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Virus:} A program that requires user action to execute and replicate, often causing damage to the local file system.
    \item \textbf{Worm:} A self-replicating program that spreads across networks automatically, often leading to resource saturation and network congestion.
    \item \textbf{Trojan:} Malicious software disguised as a legitimate program; it delivers a hidden payload that performs unauthorized actions.
    \item \textbf{Backdoor:} A method of bypassing normal authentication or access controls to gain unauthorized remote access to a system.
    \item \textbf{Ransomware:} An attack where actors encrypt a target's assets and demand payment (ransom) to restore the availability and confidentiality of the data.
\end{itemize}

\section{Social Engineering}
Technical controls alone are insufficient for total security because humans are frequently the "weakest link" in the security chain. \textbf{Social engineering} is the psychological manipulation of individuals to trick them into performing actions or disclosing confidential information.

A prominent example is \textbf{Phishing}, where an attacker sends fraudulent communications (often emails) that appear to come from a reputable source to steal sensitive data such as login credentials or credit card numbers.
